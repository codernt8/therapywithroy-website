Gender Dysphoria: What It Is, What It Feels Like and How Therapy Can Help

Roy Lam
MBACP Registered Therapist
26 October 2026 🕒 7 min read

Gender dysphoria is a term that appears often in the news and much less often in a way that helps the person living with it. This post is a plain account of what it is, how it can affect mental health, what support exists in the UK, and where therapy fits in. It is for anyone questioning their gender, anyone who experiences dysphoria, and the partners, parents and friends who want to understand.

What Gender Dysphoria Is

The NHS describes gender dysphoria as the distress or unease some people feel when their gender identity does not match the sex they were registered at birth. Two things are worth saying clearly. Being transgender or gender diverse is not a mental illness. And not everyone who is trans experiences dysphoria. The word describes the distress, not the identity.

What It Can Feel Like

It looks different for different people. For some it is a discomfort with parts of their body, such as their chest, their voice or their body hair. For others it is about how the world treats them: being addressed by the wrong name or pronoun, or being expected to fit a role that does not feel like theirs. Mirrors and photographs can become difficult. It can be constant, or it can come in waves set off by particular situations.

Day to day it often shows up as anxiety, low mood, avoiding social situations, pulling away from friends, trouble concentrating at work, or harsh self-criticism. Some people describe a background hum of unease that went on for years and was put down to something else, a theme I explored in my post on coming out later in life. Some people are non-binary or gender non-conforming and experience dysphoria in their own way, and some never use the word at all. There is no single route either. For some people, a change of name, clothes or pronouns brings relief. Others pursue medical care. Others find a way of living that sits somewhere in between. All of these are valid.

Why Mental Health Often Suffers

Dysphoria itself can be painful, but it rarely arrives alone. There are decisions about who to tell and when, fear of rejection by family, friends or colleagues, and a public conversation that can feel hostile and impossible to escape. There is also the waiting. The government’s own announcement is titled Support for adults facing staggering waits for gender services, which tells you something about how long people can be left without care.

In my view, a lot of the anxiety and low mood that trans and gender-diverse people report comes from this surrounding pressure, rather than from who they are. That matters, because it means much of the distress can be eased with the right support, even while the bigger questions are still unanswered.

If you are struggling with how you feel about your gender, or with the pressure that surrounds it, you do not need a diagnosis or a referral to talk to someone. Getting Help in the UK

For gender-related healthcare, the usual first step is a conversation with your GP. The NHS explains that for adults, a GP can refer you to an adult gender referral support service, which can then arrange an appointment at an NHS gender dysphoria clinic. It is worth checking the NHS page above for the latest, because services and waiting arrangements change.

Support for your mental health does not have to wait for that process. In England you can refer yourself to NHS talking therapies for anxiety or low mood without seeing a GP first. Switchboard offers a confidential listening service for LGBTQ+ people, and Gendered Intelligence is a trans-led charity with information and community groups. If you ever feel hopeless or think about harming yourself, please contact your GP or NHS 111, or call the Samaritans free on 116 123.

What Helps in the Meantime

Find somewhere you are seen as you are. That might be one trusted friend, an online or in-person group, or the room where you have therapy. Even one place where you are called by the right name can take the edge off the rest of the week.

Use the small things within your control. For some people, clothes, hair, a name used in safe spaces or a change of pronouns in some settings can ease day-to-day distress. You can go at your own pace, and you can change your mind.

Limit how much of the debate you take in. Constant exposure to hostile commentary is exhausting. Muting, setting time limits and taking breaks from the news can help, and my post on why you cannot stop doomscrolling has some ideas.

Look after the basics. Sleep, movement and regular meals do not solve dysphoria, but they make everything else easier to carry.

Choose who you tell, and when. Coming out is not something you owe anyone, and there is no deadline. If your family or community has different expectations, that can add another layer of difficulty, and my post on cultural identity and family expectations looks at that.

You do not have to have everything worked out to deserve support. Being unsure is a perfectly good reason to talk to someone.

How Therapy Can Help

It is worth being clear about what therapy is not. A counsellor is not a gatekeeper, and a therapy room is not somewhere you should be talked into or out of anything. The BACP’s good practice guidance on gender, sexual and relationship diversity encourages counsellors to work in an affirming way, and the ethical principles of the work stay the same whatever someone’s gender journey looks like.

When I say I work in an affirming way, I mean that I take your account of yourself seriously, I do not treat your identity as the problem, and I do not steer you towards any particular outcome. Whether you feel certain, questioning, or somewhere in between, the pace is yours.

In practice, therapy can help you make sense of what you are feeling, ease anxiety and low mood, and prepare for conversations with family, partners or an employer. It can give you somewhere to put the stress of waiting for medical care, and space to process rejection, loss or the fear of it. It is also here for people who have already transitioned and are dealing with something new, and for those who decide to pause or reverse a transition.

If Someone You Love Is Going Through This

If a partner, child or friend has told you they are experiencing gender dysphoria, you may feel confused, worried or even grief-stricken, and all of that is understandable. A few things tend to help: listening more than you speak, using the name and pronouns they ask for, and not demanding certainty from them that they may not have yet. Your own feelings are valid too, and they are best worked through with your own support rather than put onto the person who has trusted you. Family and couples sessions can also help everyone find a way through together.
