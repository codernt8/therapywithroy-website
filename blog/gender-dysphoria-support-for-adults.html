Living With Gender Dysphoria: What It Is and How Therapy Can Support You

Roy Lam
MBACP Registered Therapist
13 November 2026 🕒 6 min read

Trans Awareness Week begins today, and it seems the right moment to talk about something that many adults live with quietly: the distress that can come when the gender you know yourself to be does not match the one you were registered as at birth. This is for adults who are going through that, including those who are not sure yet whether the word fits them, and who would like to understand what is happening and what kind of support exists.

It is written for you, not about you. You do not need to have your story worked out to read it.

What Gender Dysphoria Is, and What It Is Not

The NHS describes gender dysphoria as feeling distressed or uncomfortable for a long time because the gender you feel you are, your gender identity, is different from the sex you were registered as at birth. The feeling of those two being different is known as gender incongruence.

The two words are not the same thing. Mind explains that gender incongruence is the mismatch itself, while gender dysphoria is the distress you might feel because of it, and that not everyone who experiences incongruence will also experience dysphoria. People also have different views about whether “dysphoria” is a helpful term. Some find it useful for naming what they feel. Others find it stigmatising, because it is also used for a diagnosis.

One point matters above all the others. Mind is clear that being trans or non-binary is not a mental health problem, and that it does not cause mental health problems. The distress is real and it deserves attention, but it is not evidence that something is wrong with who you are.

What It Can Feel Like

The NHS says these feelings can make it hard to enjoy everyday life and may affect your mental health. You may feel depressed or anxious, or find yourself having thoughts of harming yourself. Some people describe a constant background strain, a sense of never being quite at ease in their body, in how others address them, or in the roles they are expected to play. Others feel it more sharply at particular moments, such as a mirror, a form, a family occasion or a photograph.

A good deal of the distress is not about gender alone. It is also about how the world responds. Mind lists experiences that can lead to poor mental health for trans and non-binary people, including transphobia, difficult experiences of coming out, exclusion from LGBTQ+ spaces, internalised transphobia, barriers to healthcare and legal gender recognition, and discrimination or abuse. Carrying those on top of the dysphoria itself can be exhausting.

If things feel too much right now, you can call Samaritans free on 116 123, any time, day or night.

Waiting for Care

For many adults, one of the hardest parts is the wait. The NHS says to start by seeing a GP, and that waiting times to be assessed at a gender dysphoria clinic are currently very long. It also notes that some private gender clinics have shorter waiting times, though you pay for any treatment. For adults, the NHS lists the possible treatments as hormone therapy, voice and communication therapy, talking therapy and counselling, and surgery.

Waiting can leave life feeling on hold. You may be making decisions about work, relationships and how open to be, without knowing when or whether medical support will arrive. That is a heavy thing to carry, and support for the emotional side of it does not have to wait for a clinic appointment.

How Therapy Can Help

My approach is affirming. That means I take your sense of your own gender seriously, and the work is about helping you explore what feels right for you and make your own decisions, at your own pace. I am not steering you towards any particular outcome or away from one. My role is to support you emotionally.

I should also be clear about what I am not. I am a counsellor and therapist, not a gender clinic. I do not diagnose gender dysphoria, and I do not prescribe or arrange medical treatment. What I can offer is a steady, confidential space to talk, think and feel your way through whatever you are carrying.

In practice that might mean working through anxiety or low mood, thinking about how and whether to come out to the people in your life, making sense of difficult reactions from family or friends, coping with the wait, or simply having somewhere you do not have to explain yourself. Therapy should never try to change or suppress your gender identity. Mind describes conversion “therapy” as a form of abuse and says plainly that you do not need to be “cured” or changed.

Your gender is not the problem. The distress is real, and you deserve support for it.

Other Places to Find Support

You may want more than one source of support, and that is sensible. If you are 18 or over, the NHS says you can refer yourself to an NHS talking therapies service for certain mental health conditions such as depression or anxiety. Mind’s list of contacts for LGBTQ+ mental health includes MindLine Trans+ (0300 330 5468), a free, confidential listening service for people who identify as trans or non-binary and their friends and families, and the Clare Project, which offers online information and groups for trans, non-binary and intersex adults.

However you feel about the language, and wherever you are in your own understanding, you do not have to have it settled before you ask for help. Starting with a conversation is enough.
