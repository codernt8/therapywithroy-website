Grief & Loss

Grief Doesn’t Follow a Timeline: What LGBTQ+ People Know About Loss That Others Don’t

Roy Lam
MBACP Registered Therapist
12 March 2026 🕒 6 min read

There is a particular kind of grief that nobody makes a card for. Not the grief that comes with a funeral, a condolence book, or colleagues quietly covering your work for a week. The grief that follows the death of someone whose relationship to you was never fully acknowledged. The grief of losing the community you built, because they were the only people who ever really knew you.

For many LGBTQ+ people, loss arrives in forms that the world around them doesn’t always have language for. And when grief doesn’t fit a recognised shape, it can be much harder to process, and much harder to ask for help.

This post explores why grief in LGBTQ+ communities is often more complicated, what disenfranchised grief actually means, and why talking to a therapist can make a genuine difference.

The Myth of the Five Stages

Most people have heard of the five stages of grief: denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance. The model, developed by psychiatrist Elisabeth Kübler-Ross in 1969, was never intended as a linear checklist. But that is how it entered popular culture.

The reality is that grief is not a process with a clear endpoint. It does not move in order. It circles back. It goes quiet for months and then surfaces at a supermarket checkout or on a Tuesday morning for no apparent reason. It can feel disproportionate to outsiders who didn’t understand the relationship, which can leave the person grieving feeling confused about whether what they’re feeling is even valid.

It is. Grief is always valid, including when the loss is one that others don’t fully see.

What Disenfranchised Grief Means

Disenfranchised grief is a term for grief that is not openly acknowledged, publicly mourned, or socially supported. The loss is real, but the grieving person receives little or no recognition for it.

LGBTQ+ people encounter disenfranchised grief more often, and in more forms, than most. Some examples:

  • The death of a chosen family member. A close friend, a drag mother, a chosen sibling. Someone who was not a legal relative, and whose significance in your life was not understood by those around you. Bereavement leave doesn’t apply. There is no formal structure for the mourning.
  • The end of a same-sex relationship kept private. Where a partner’s family took back access to the home, the belongings, even the funeral arrangements. The surviving partner may have no legal standing and no social permission to grieve openly.
  • Community loss. Many LGBTQ+ people, particularly gay men who came of age during the AIDS crisis, carry a grief load that was never fully processed. Switchboard, the LGBTQ+ support charity, continues to provide support for people navigating this kind of layered, ongoing loss.
  • Estrangement from family of origin. The loss of family relationships through coming out, rejection, or simply growing apart is a form of grief that rarely gets named. There is no death, no ceremony, and often no understanding from people outside the experience.

Why LGBTQ+ Grief Is Often Harder to Process

Beyond disenfranchisement, there are structural reasons why grief can be more complex for LGBTQ+ people.

Minority stress, the chronic pressure of navigating a world that is not always safe or accepting, means that many LGBTQ+ people already carry a higher baseline of anxiety and emotional load. When grief arrives on top of that, it can feel overwhelming in ways that are difficult to explain to people who haven’t experienced it.

There is also the question of support. Finding a therapist who understands LGBTQ+ lives, who doesn’t require you to explain your relationships or translate your experience before you can begin to process it, matters enormously. The NHS encourages anyone struggling with grief to seek talking therapy when it feels too heavy to carry alone. The BACP recognises bereavement as one of the most common reasons people seek therapy, and acknowledges that grief can take many forms. Organisations such as LGBT Health and Wellbeing and Galop offer specialist support for LGBTQ+ people navigating mental health challenges, including loss and trauma.

Grief That Resurfaces, and Why That’s Normal

One of the most disorienting things about grief is how it can appear to settle, and then resurface years later. A bereavement from ten years ago can feel raw again after a new loss. The grief of family estrangement can intensify at milestones, a wedding, a diagnosis, a birthday, when the absence of certain people becomes especially visible.

This is not a sign that something has gone wrong with your healing. It is a sign that the loss mattered.

Cruse Bereavement Support, the UK’s leading bereavement charity, describes grief as something that changes rather than disappears. Many people find their grief shifts in shape and weight over time, particularly when they have space to process it properly.

The grief that isn’t named is still grief. And grief that isn’t witnessed is often the heaviest kind to carry.

When to Consider Therapy

Grief doesn’t require a threshold to be reached before it’s worth talking to someone. But the following may be signs that some support would help:

  • You are finding it difficult to function day to day: sleeping, eating, concentrating, keeping up with work or relationships.
  • You feel like you cannot talk to the people around you, either because they don’t understand the relationship you’ve lost, or because they expect you to be “over it” by now.
  • You are using alcohol, substances, or other behaviours to manage the feeling.
  • You feel stuck, as though time is passing but the grief is not shifting at all.
  • You are carrying multiple losses at once: estrangement, relationship endings, and community losses alongside a bereavement.

Therapy doesn’t fix grief. Nothing does. But it gives you a space where the loss is taken seriously, in all its complexity, and where you are not alone with it.
