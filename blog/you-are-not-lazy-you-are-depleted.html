You’re Not Lazy. You’re Depleted: Understanding Why So Many People Can’t Switch Off

Roy Lam
MBACP Registered Therapist
10 March 2026 🕒 6 min read

Something has quietly shifted in the way people talk about tiredness. It is no longer just physical. It is the kind of exhaustion that persists even after a full night’s sleep, that makes small decisions feel enormous, and that no amount of doing less seems to fix. If that resonates, this isn’t about a lack of willpower. It’s about what happens to the body and mind under sustained pressure.

The NHS recently launched a major campaign highlighting that an estimated 9.4 million adults in England are living with a common mental health condition, many of whom have never sought support. At the same time, Mental Health UK’s Burnout Report 2026 found that 91% of UK adults reported experiencing high or extreme stress in the past year, with just one in four saying they feel mentally healthy at work.

These are not isolated statistics. They are describing something most of us feel but rarely name: a collective depletion, quietly accumulating.

What “Can’t Switch Off” Actually Means

Most people assume they can’t switch off because they have too much to do. And sometimes that’s true. But for a significant number of people, the inability to rest runs deeper than a busy schedule. It’s a nervous system that has been in a state of low-level alert for so long that “safe” no longer feels like a setting it knows how to return to.

This is sometimes called chronic stress arousal. The body’s threat response, designed for short bursts of danger, gets stuck in the “on” position. And when that happens, rest doesn’t feel restorative. It feels uncomfortable, even suspicious. There’s a nagging sense that you should be doing something, that stillness is a risk you can’t afford.

Over time, this produces a very particular kind of exhaustion: one that doesn’t respond to holidays, doesn’t lift on weekends, and tends to worsen in the evenings when there’s finally nothing left to distract you from it.

Why High-Functioning People Are Often the Last to Notice

There is a particular trap that affects people who are, by most external measures, managing well. They get up, they go to work, they meet their responsibilities, they show up for the people who need them. And they do all of this while running on empty, because they have become very skilled at performing capability.

BACP therapists reflecting on mental health in 2026 noted that many clients are presenting not in crisis but in a quiet, sustained state of overwhelm: high-functioning on the surface, depleted underneath. This pattern isn’t confined to any one demographic. It shows up across genders, professions and life stages. What it tends to have in common is a long history of pushing through, and a deep reluctance to ask for help.

Part of that reluctance is cultural: we have learned to value productivity and stoicism, and to treat needing rest as a kind of weakness. Part of it is practical: when you are holding a lot together, stopping, even briefly, feels dangerous.

The Signs Worth Paying Attention To

You might be more depleted than you realise if:

  • Sleep leaves you feeling no better, or you lie awake with a busy, racing mind despite exhaustion
  • Small decisions feel disproportionately difficult
  • You feel irritable or short-tempered in ways that don’t feel like you
  • You’ve stopped enjoying things you used to look forward to
  • You feel a persistent, low-level sense of dread without a clear cause
  • You tell yourself things will get better when this particular busy period is over, and then the next one begins

None of these on their own are cause for alarm. But together, persisting over weeks or months, they are your body and mind asking for something different.

This Isn’t About Doing Less. It’s About Feeling Safe Enough to Rest.

One of the most common misconceptions about depletion is that the solution is simply to reduce activity: take a week off, do less, slow down. And while rest matters, it only works if the nervous system is in a state where rest is possible.

For many people, what needs to shift isn’t the schedule, it’s the underlying sense of threat. The belief, often unconscious, that they are only as valuable as they are useful. That stopping means falling behind. That if they let themselves feel how tired they really are, something will collapse.

For many people, what needs to shift isn’t the schedule. It’s the underlying sense that stopping means something will collapse.

Therapy offers a space to explore where these patterns come from, not to dissect the past endlessly, but to understand what’s keeping the nervous system on alert in the present. Approaches like CBT can help identify and shift the thought patterns that drive chronic overextension. Emotion-focused work can help people reconnect with what they’re actually feeling underneath the performance of coping. Over time, it becomes possible to rest without guilt, not because everything is resolved, but because you have developed a different relationship with yourself.

You Are Allowed to Need More Than You’re Getting

The NHS talking therapies campaign is a reminder that support exists, is accessible, and works. Self-referral is free and available across England, without needing to go through a GP. But beyond the practical, there is something worth sitting with: the idea that you don’t have to earn the right to feel better.

Depletion is not a character flaw. It is what happens when a person has been giving more than they’ve been replenishing, for longer than the body and mind can sustain. If you recognise yourself in this, that recognition is the beginning of something.
