Why Perfectionism Is Making You Anxious (And What to Do About It)

Roy Lam
MBACP Registered Therapist
18 March 2026 🕒 7 min read

Perfectionism is often worn as a badge of honour. People describe themselves as perfectionists with a degree of pride, as though it explains their work ethic or their high standards. But a growing body of research tells a different story: perfectionism is one of the most reliable predictors of anxiety, depression, burnout, and insomnia. Understanding why, and what to do about it, is some of the most useful work I do with clients.

A study published in January 2026 in the Australian Journal of Psychology found that perfectionistic concerns, the kind rooted in fear of failure and harsh self-evaluation, were directly linked to higher psychological distress and lower flourishing. Crucially, self-esteem was identified as a key mediating factor: when perfectionism erodes self-worth, the mental health impact becomes significantly worse. This is not a minor finding. It has direct implications for how perfectionism should be addressed in therapy.

Meanwhile, a BBC Future review of wellbeing research highlighted perfectionism as one of the clearest modifiable risk factors for poor mental health, linking it to social anxiety, obsessive tendencies, chronic fatigue, and elevated suicide risk in severe cases. These are not fringe findings. Perfectionism is mainstream, it is common, and it is genuinely harmful when left unaddressed.

What Perfectionism Actually Is

Most people think of perfectionism as simply having high standards. That is not quite right. Researchers distinguish between two distinct forms, and understanding the difference matters.

The first is perfectionistic strivings: a genuine drive to do things well, to improve, to meet challenging goals. In moderation, this can be adaptive. It can fuel persistence and achievement without necessarily causing harm.

The second, and far more damaging, is perfectionistic concerns: an excessive preoccupation with mistakes, a fear of failure, harsh self-criticism when goals are not met, and a sense that anything less than perfect is unacceptable. This is the kind that quietly poisons daily life. The goal posts keep moving, so the sense of achievement never arrives. Every shortfall, however minor, becomes evidence of inadequacy. Over time, this creates a relentless internal pressure that is exhausting to live with.

In my work with clients, I see perfectionism show up in ways people do not always recognise as perfectionism. Procrastination is one of the most common: not laziness, but avoidance of starting something because the fear of not doing it well enough is overwhelming. Over-preparation is another: spending four hours on a task that warranted forty minutes. Then there is the inability to delegate, the difficulty accepting praise, and the persistent sense that no matter what you accomplish, it is never quite enough.

The Anxiety Connection

Perfectionism and anxiety are deeply intertwined, and it is worth being specific about why.

When self-worth becomes dependent on performance, the stakes of every task escalate. A piece of work is no longer just a piece of work: it is a referendum on whether you are capable, valuable, or worthy of approval. That cognitive load is exhausting, and it creates a near-constant background hum of tension and vigilance.

Mind’s guidance on anxiety describes the way anxious thinking locks people into cycles of worry and avoidance. Perfectionism feeds directly into this cycle. The worry is about not being good enough. The avoidance is of tasks that might confirm that fear. The temporary relief of avoidance reinforces the cycle, making it harder to break without outside support.

The physical dimension matters too. The NHS describes how chronic anxiety manifests in the body: tension, disturbed sleep, difficulty concentrating, and physical symptoms including headaches and digestive issues. Many of my clients arrive with these symptoms and have not connected them to the way they are relating to their own performance and self-worth.

Perfectionism is not about having high standards. It is about tying your worth to outcomes you can never fully control.

Where Perfectionism Comes From

Perfectionism rarely appears from nowhere. It tends to have roots, and understanding them is part of how therapy helps.

For many people, perfectionism developed as an adaptive response to an environment where love, approval, or safety felt conditional on performance. If praise was scarce unless results were excellent, or if criticism was frequent and harsh, a child learns to try to control outcomes as a way of managing emotional risk. The strategy works, in a limited way, for a while. Over time, it becomes a rigid pattern that outlasts the context it was created for.

Cultural and professional environments can amplify this. In workplaces that reward overwork and treat mistakes as unacceptable, perfectionism is not just tolerated: it is actively cultivated. Social media compounds it further, presenting a curated world in which everyone else appears to be achieving effortlessly, which makes any personal shortfall feel even more significant.

None of this makes perfectionism the person’s fault. But it does mean there is something to understand and work with, rather than simply push through.

How Therapy Helps

Perfectionism is one of the areas where I find CBT particularly useful. Research published by the American Counseling Association highlights cognitive-behavioural approaches as effective in identifying and challenging the thought patterns that sustain perfectionism: the all-or-nothing thinking, the catastrophising about mistakes, and the conditional self-worth that keeps the anxiety cycle running.

In practice, this means examining the rules and assumptions that drive perfectionistic behaviour. Where did this standard come from? What does it mean if it is not met? Is the evidence for that interpretation as strong as it feels? These are not rhetorical questions. They are genuine enquiries that, with the right support, lead to real shifts in how a person relates to themselves and their performance.

Emotion-focused approaches add a different layer. Rather than just challenging the thoughts, they help explore the emotional experiences underneath: the fear of rejection, the grief of not feeling good enough, the exhaustion of never being at rest. When those emotions are processed and understood, the rigid strategies built around them tend to loosen.

Therapy might be worth considering if you notice:

  • Difficulty starting tasks because the fear of not doing them perfectly keeps getting in the way.
  • A persistent inner critic that focuses on what went wrong rather than what went well.
  • Work or performance dominating your sense of self-worth, so that a bad day at work becomes a bad day as a person.
  • Exhaustion that does not improve with rest, because the internal pressure does not switch off.
  • Difficulty accepting help or delegating, because no one else will do it well enough.
  • Feeling like you are never quite enough, even when the objective evidence suggests otherwise.

These are recognisable patterns. They are also patterns that respond well to the right kind of therapeutic work. Finding a therapist is a good starting point, and the BACP therapist directory allows you to search for accredited practitioners by location, specialism, and approach.
