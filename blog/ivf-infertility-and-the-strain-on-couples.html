The Hidden Strain of IVF: Infertility, Identity and Staying Close as a Couple

Roy Lam
MBACP Registered Therapist
26 October 2026 🕒 7 min read

In a Guardian interview published this weekend, Greg Wise talks about how he and his wife, Emma Thompson, coped when IVF did not work. The headline carries a line that will be painfully familiar to many couples who have been through fertility treatment: “She was not a proper woman and I was not a proper man.”

Whatever the details of any one couple’s story, that sentence captures something important. Greg Wise’s interview in the Guardian is a reminder that fertility problems do not only disrupt a plan. They reach into how you see yourself, how you see each other, and what you thought your life would look like.

Why IVF Is So Stressful

IVF is rarely a single event. It is usually a long run of appointments, medication, scans, procedures and waiting, repeated over months or sometimes years, and often squeezed in around work and paid for out of savings. Each cycle ends with a result that can lift or flatten you in a single phone call. The wait after an embryo transfer is especially hard, because there is nothing left to do except hope.

Much of it is out of your hands, which is difficult for anyone who copes with stress by taking action. And the strain lands on two people at once, who may be experiencing it very differently. The NHS information on infertility explains that it can be down to either partner, to both, or to no clear cause at all. Whichever it is, both of you are living with the consequences.

When the Diagnosis Lands on One of You

If a medical cause is found in one partner, that person can end up carrying it like a verdict. Many describe feeling less of a man or less of a woman, as if their body has failed at something it was supposed to do naturally. There can be shame, a habit of comparing yourself with friends who conceived easily, and a quiet fear that your partner would be better off with someone else.

These feelings are common because ideas about manhood and womanhood are so tied up with fertility. Those ideas do not hold up, and they say nothing about your worth, your attractiveness or your ability to love. But feelings do not always follow logic, and when you are in the middle of treatment it can be hard to remember that. Even when no cause is found, many people search for someone to blame, and often settle on themselves.

When You Are the Partner Who Feels Helpless

The other partner often has a different and less visible struggle. If it is not your body or your diagnosis, there is not much you can do except be there, and that can feel like very little. You may be watching someone you love go through injections, side effects and disappointment, and feel powerless to make any of it easier.

You may also feel guilty for grieving, as if your loss counts for less. Many partners decide they have to be the strong one, so they say nothing about their own sadness. From the outside, that silence can look like distance. In reality, you are both mourning the same future, and each of you may be waiting for the other to speak first.

The Physical Side: Hormones and Mood

Treatment usually involves medication to stimulate the ovaries to produce more eggs. People commonly report bloating, tiredness, headaches and mood changes while taking it, and the emotional effects can pile on top of anxiety that is already high. Knowing that some of the tearfulness or irritability may be chemical can help the person having treatment feel less alarmed by it, and can help a partner avoid taking every sharp moment personally.

In a small number of cases the ovaries respond too strongly to stimulation, a condition called ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome. It needs prompt medical attention, so if you have severe pain, rapid swelling or breathlessness during treatment, contact your clinic or NHS 111 straight away rather than waiting it out. Your clinic should talk you through the side effects and risks before you start.

When It Does Not Work

When a cycle fails, or a pregnancy ends, the feeling is grief. It is the loss of a baby who existed in your imagination, along with the future you had pictured around them. Because there is often nothing to mark it, no funeral and sometimes no one who even knew you were trying, this kind of grief can be lonely. Well-meaning comments such as “just relax”, “you can always try again” or “at least you’re young” can make it harder to say how bad it feels.

The grief often builds with each cycle, and it sits alongside decisions that can pull a couple in different directions: how many more attempts to make, how much to spend, whether to consider donor options, and when to stop. It is common for one person to want to continue while the other is ready to stop. Neither of you is wrong, and it is a conversation that deserves care and time.

Fertility treatment asks a great deal of a relationship. It helps to remember that you are two people facing the same difficulty, not one patient and one bystander.

Staying Close as a Couple

Expect to cope differently. One of you may want to talk it through constantly while the other goes quiet. Both are ways of managing something painful, and understanding that can take the heat out of misunderstandings.

Agree when you talk about treatment. If every dinner and every weekend becomes a conversation about the next step, there is no rest. Setting aside a regular time to check in, and leaving other times free, can protect the rest of your life together.

Say the thing you are afraid to say. “I feel like I have let you down.” “I feel useless.” “I am scared this will never happen.” Naming these feelings out loud often shrinks them, and it gives your partner the chance to respond with care rather than guess.

Protect something that is not about fertility. When sex becomes scheduled and bodies become projects, intimacy can suffer. Make some room for time together that has nothing to do with treatment, even if it is only a walk or a film.

Decide how you will decide. Agree in advance when you will review things, for example after each cycle rather than in the middle of one, and what you will do if you disagree. It is much easier to have that conversation before an emotional moment than during it.

Tell people what helps. Friends and family often do not know what to say. You can choose who to tell, and you can say what you need, whether that is practical help, a listening ear or simply not being asked every week.

Getting Support

You do not have to wait for treatment to end to get support. Fertility clinics should be able to offer counselling, and the HFEA’s page on getting emotional support is a good place to start. Fertility Network UK is a charity that offers a support line and peer groups for people at every stage of the journey. Your GP can help too, and in England you can refer yourself to NHS talking therapies if you are struggling with anxiety or low mood. If you ever feel hopeless or think about harming yourself, please contact your GP or NHS 111, or call the Samaritans free on 116 123.

How Therapy Can Help

Individual therapy gives you a place to say what you may be holding back at home, perhaps because you are trying to protect your partner. It can help with shame, anxiety, the sense of failure, and the grief that comes with each setback, and with the bigger question of who you are when the life you planned changes shape.

Couples therapy offers a different kind of space: a place where both of you can speak honestly without it turning into another argument, and where you can learn to hear what the other person is really carrying. It can help you make decisions together, including the hardest ones, and find your way back to each other when treatment has made you feel like teammates in a project rather than partners. Therapy can start before treatment, during it, after it, or when you decide to stop.
