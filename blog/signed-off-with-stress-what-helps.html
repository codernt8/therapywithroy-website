Signed Off With Stress: What Actually Helps When You’re Off Work for Your Mental Health

Roy Lam
MBACP Registered Therapist
9 October 2026 🕒 7 min read

Being signed off work for your mental health rarely feels like the rest it is supposed to be. For many people it feels more like limbo: guilt about colleagues picking up the slack, dread every time an email notification appears, and a nagging question in the background about whether you’ll ever feel ready to go back.

If that sounds familiar, you are far from alone, and how you spend this time matters more than most people realise.

The Numbers Behind the Headlines

In September the government published an update to its Keep Britain Working review, setting out plans for what it calls Britain’s first Workplace Health System. The figures are striking. Around 300,000 people with health conditions leave work every year. Someone who is off for four to six weeks has a 96% chance of returning, but fewer than half of people return after a year away. The report also highlights “long silent periods on fit notes” as part of the problem, with prolonged absence and no contact making things harder, not easier.

Behind those numbers is a simple, human point. Time off can be exactly what someone needs, but time off spent in isolation, dread and silence can quietly make the road back longer.

Why Time Off Doesn’t Always Feel Like Rest

Before becoming a counsellor, I spent over 30 years in senior Human Resources roles across banking, aviation, luxury retail and hospitality, so I have seen these situations from the employer’s side as well as, now, from the therapy room. One of the most common things people describe is that the first week or two off is dominated by guilt and adrenaline rather than recovery. You may still be mentally at your desk, replaying conversations, checking emails you promised yourself you wouldn’t, or worrying about what people are saying.

That is not a sign you are doing it wrong. It is what a stressed nervous system does when it finally stops. But it helps to know that recovery usually needs a bit of intention, not just the absence of work.

Using the Time Well

Rest first, then rhythm. The early days are often about sleep, basic routines and letting your body come down from a prolonged state of stress. After that, gently rebuilding a daily rhythm, getting up at a similar time, getting outside, moving your body a little, tends to help mood and confidence more than long stretches of unstructured time.

Get support for what’s underneath. Being signed off treats the immediate crisis, not whatever led to it. If you’re in England, you can refer yourself to NHS talking therapies for difficulties like anxiety, low mood and worry without seeing a GP first, and your employer may offer an employee assistance programme or private support.

Decide how you want to stay in touch. Going completely silent can make returning feel more daunting, but you are also entitled to boundaries. Mind’s guidance on returning to work suggests staying in touch with people at work during your time off if you find it helps. Agreeing how and how often contact will happen, and who it will be with, can take a lot of the dread out of it.

Planning Your Return

When you start to feel steadier, it helps to think about going back as a process rather than a single Monday morning. Mind suggests asking to return gradually, working part-time or on certain days to begin with, and creating a plan with your manager for your first weeks back. Your employer may also be able to involve occupational health to help build that plan, and to consider small changes that make your job more manageable.

Acas guidance describes a return to work meeting as often “just an informal chat” between you and your manager, and makes clear that you don’t have to share personal information you’re not comfortable sharing. It can help to go in having thought about what you need: a phased start, a temporary change in workload, regular check-ins, or clarity about who to go to if things start to slip.

Going back to work isn’t the finish line. The real question is what needs to be different this time, so you’re not back in the same place six months from now.

How Therapy Helps

Most people who are signed off with stress already know the practical advice. What is harder is understanding why you ended up here, and what to change so it doesn’t happen again. Therapy gives you space to look at the patterns underneath: perhaps a habit of saying yes to everything, a belief that your worth depends on performance, difficulty asking for help, or a workplace dynamic that simply isn’t sustainable.

It can also help with the return itself: working through the anxiety of that first day back, rehearsing difficult conversations with a manager, and deciding what boundaries you want to hold. And sometimes it helps with a bigger question, whether this job, or this career, is still the right fit for you.
