Attachment Styles and Relationships: Why We Love the Way We Do

Roy Lam
MBACP Registered Therapist
16 March 2026 🕒 7 min read

Have you ever noticed that you tend to fall into the same patterns in relationships, no matter how different the person is? You might find yourself becoming anxious when a partner seems distant, or pulling away when things start to feel too close. These patterns usually have deep roots, and understanding them is one of the most useful things you can do for your relationships and your mental health.

New research from Purdue University, published in February 2026, confirms what therapists have observed for decades: attachment styles have a significant influence on relationship satisfaction. Couples where both partners had anxious or avoidant attachment patterns reported the lowest levels of happiness in their marriages. Those with secure attachment styles consistently fared best. But the findings also carried a note of hope: attachment patterns can shift, and therapy is one of the most effective ways to shift them.

In this post, I want to explain what attachment theory actually means in practice, how your early experiences shape the way you relate to others as an adult, and what you can do if your attachment style is getting in the way of the relationships you want.

Where Attachment Styles Come From

Attachment theory was developed by the British psychiatrist John Bowlby in the 1960s and 1970s, and later expanded by the developmental psychologist Mary Ainsworth. The central insight is simple but profound: the way we are cared for in infancy creates a template for how we expect relationships to work throughout our lives.

When caregivers are consistently available, responsive, and emotionally attuned, children develop what is known as a secure attachment: a foundational belief that they are worthy of love and that others can be trusted. When caregivers are inconsistent, unavailable, or overwhelming, children adapt by developing strategies to manage the uncertainty. These adaptations become the attachment patterns that follow them into adulthood.

As Psychology Today’s overview of attachment theory explains, these early patterns are not fixed, but they are persistent. They become the lens through which we interpret our closest relationships, shaping what we expect from partners, how we respond to conflict, and how comfortable we are with closeness and vulnerability.

The Four Main Attachment Styles

Attachment researchers generally describe four patterns in adults. Most people recognise themselves in more than one, and the patterns can shift depending on the relationship and life circumstances.

  • Secure attachment: A comfortable capacity for both intimacy and independence. People with this style tend to communicate their needs clearly, handle conflict constructively, and trust that their partner will be there for them without becoming preoccupied by doubt.
  • Anxious (or preoccupied) attachment: A heightened need for reassurance, combined with a persistent fear of abandonment. People with this style often interpret ambiguous signals negatively, become distressed when partners seem unavailable, and may come across as needy or demanding, even when they do not want to be.
  • Avoidant (or dismissing) attachment: A tendency to prioritise self-reliance and emotional distance. People with this style often find intimacy uncomfortable, withdraw when relationships become intense, and may dismiss or minimise their own emotional needs as well as those of others.
  • Disorganised (or fearful-avoidant) attachment: A mix of longing for closeness and fear of it. This pattern often develops in the context of early experiences that were frightening or unpredictable. It can make relationships feel both desperately wanted and deeply unsafe at the same time.

None of these styles is a diagnosis or a fixed identity. They are patterns, learned responses that made sense in the context where they developed. And that matters, because what was learned can be revisited.

How Attachment Patterns Show Up in Adult Relationships

Attachment styles do not just affect romantic relationships. They shape friendships, professional relationships, and the relationship you have with yourself. But they are often most visible, and most activated, in close partnerships.

Someone with an anxious attachment style might find themselves constantly checking for signs that their partner is losing interest, sending follow-up messages when they do not hear back quickly, or struggling to believe that a relationship is secure even when there is little objective reason to doubt it. The underlying fear is always the same: that they are not enough, that they will be left.

Someone with an avoidant style might struggle to understand why their partner needs so much reassurance, feel smothered by emotional demands they find hard to meet, or notice that they are most comfortable in relationships that stay relatively surface-level. When things start to feel really close, something in them pulls back.

What makes this particularly complex is what researchers call the anxious-avoidant trap: a pairing where one person’s anxiety activates the other’s avoidance, and vice versa, creating a cycle that both people find frustrating and painful but struggle to escape. The Purdue research found that this dynamic, where both partners bring insecure attachment histories, produced the lowest relationship satisfaction scores.

Attachment patterns are not a life sentence. They are a starting point for understanding, and understanding is where real change begins.

Can You Change Your Attachment Style?

Yes. The evidence is clear on this, and it is one of the most important things I find myself saying to clients who have read about attachment theory and concluded, sometimes with a sense of despair, that they are simply “anxiously attached” and that is that.

Attachment patterns are not fixed in adulthood. They can shift through a sustained experience of being in a safe, reliable, and emotionally attuned relationship, whether that is with a partner, a close friend, or a therapist. This is sometimes called “earned security”: the development of a more secure internal model through repeated corrective relational experiences.

Therapy is one of the most direct routes to this kind of change. Relate, the UK’s leading relationship support charity, offers both individual and couples counselling to help people understand and work through the patterns that are affecting their relationships. Psychotherapy approaches including Emotion-Focused Therapy (EFT), which I draw on in my practice, have a strong evidence base specifically in the area of attachment and relationships.

In my work, the process often starts with helping people simply name the pattern they are in. That in itself can be a relief. When you understand that your anxiety in relationships is not a character flaw but an adaptive response developed much earlier in your life, the self-criticism usually softens. And once the self-criticism softens, there is more room to do something different.

Signs That Attachment Might Be Worth Exploring

Therapy around attachment is worth considering if you notice any of these patterns in your relationships:

  • You find yourself repeatedly drawn to the same type of person, even when the relationship follows a predictable and painful pattern.
  • You struggle with conflict, either by avoiding it at almost any cost, or by escalating quickly in ways that feel out of proportion.
  • Intimacy feels unsafe, whether that means emotional closeness, vulnerability, or physical affection.
  • You frequently feel let down by people you are close to, even when they describe themselves as trying to be there for you.
  • You find it hard to ask for what you need in a relationship, either because you fear rejection or because you are not sure what you need at all.
  • Relationships tend to end in similar ways, leaving you with a sense of deja vu rather than understanding.

These are not signs that something is fundamentally broken. They are signs that there is a pattern worth understanding. Mind’s guidance on loneliness and connection notes that the quality of our relationships is one of the most powerful factors in mental health overall, which is why this work tends to have wide-ranging effects beyond the relationship itself.

Working Through Attachment in Therapy

I work with adults across a range of relationship concerns: patterns of anxiety or avoidance in partnerships, the aftermath of painful break-ups, difficulties with trust or intimacy, and the longer-term effects of difficult early experiences that show up in how people relate to others today.

The modalities I draw on, including CBT, Emotion-Focused Therapy, and Bowen Family Systems Theory, are particularly well suited to this work. Bowen’s family systems approach, in particular, helps to trace relationship patterns across generations, making it possible to understand not just what you do in relationships, but where it came from and how it can change.

The Relate website also has a useful collection of self-help resources for anyone wanting to start exploring their relationship patterns before committing to therapy. It is a good first step, though for patterns that feel entrenched, working with a therapist tends to create more durable change.
