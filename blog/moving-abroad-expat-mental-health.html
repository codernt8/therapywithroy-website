Life Transitions & Expat Life

The Hidden Emotional Cost of Moving Abroad: A Guide for Expats

Roy Lam
MBACP Registered Therapist
17 March 2026 🕒 7 min read

Moving abroad is one of the biggest decisions a person can make. The logistics dominate the months before departure: visas, shipping, new bank accounts, new phone numbers. What rarely gets the same attention is the emotional reality that arrives once the boxes are unpacked and the novelty begins to fade.

According to the InterNations Expat Insider survey, which draws on responses from over 10,000 expats worldwide each year, loneliness and difficulty building new social networks consistently rank among the top challenges of living abroad, cutting across nationalities, professions, and destinations. The excitement of the move is real. So is what comes after.

I work with clients who have relocated internationally, and the patterns I see are remarkably consistent. There is often a honeymoon period, sometimes lasting months, when everything feels fresh and full of possibility. Then comes a quieter, harder phase: the realisation that you cannot quite find your footing, that your social life takes years to rebuild rather than months, and that the version of yourself that existed back home no longer has a clear shape in this new context.

This post is for anyone who has moved abroad and found the emotional experience harder than expected, and for those preparing for a move who want to go in with a clearer picture of what relocation really involves.

Why Moving Abroad Is Harder Than It Looks

The dominant cultural narrative around living abroad is a positive one. It is adventure, growth, broadened horizons. And all of that can be true. But it coexists with losses that rarely get acknowledged: the loss of easy, familiar friendships built over years; the loss of the professional status or network you worked hard to establish; the loss of the language and cultural shorthand that makes everyday life feel effortless.

Psychologists describe this as “cumulative loss”: when a series of individually manageable changes occur simultaneously, the combined effect can feel overwhelming. Relocating abroad tends to disrupt almost every domain of life at once. Your social network, your sense of professional identity, your daily routines, your relationship to language, even your sense of humour, all change at the same time.

Mind’s guidance on loneliness notes that social disconnection is one of the most significant risk factors for poor mental health, on a par with well-established physical health risks. For expats, that disconnection is not a personal failing: it is a structural consequence of relocating to a place where you have no existing relationships and where every interaction requires more effort than it would at home.

The Identity Question Nobody Warns You About

One of the most disorienting aspects of living abroad is what happens to your sense of self. Back home, your identity is partly constructed by the people who know you: friends who remember who you were at 22, colleagues who have seen you navigate difficult situations, family members whose understanding of you, however imperfect, is at least deep. Move abroad and that scaffolding disappears.

You become, in a sense, whoever you can communicate yourself to be in a new context. This can be liberating: it offers the chance to reinvent parts of yourself that felt stuck. But it can also be exhausting and destabilising, particularly if the new environment does not offer many opportunities to be known at depth.

For those who move abroad with a partner, this dynamic can put significant pressure on the relationship. When a couple becomes each other’s primary source of social and emotional support, a level of strain emerges that the relationship was not designed to carry alone. I work with couples navigating exactly this pattern, and it is one of the more common reasons people in expat communities seek therapy.

The hardest part of moving abroad is often not the practical adjustment. It is finding out who you are when the people who knew you are no longer around.

Stress, Anxiety, and the Pressure to Be Fine

There is a specific kind of pressure that expats face, which is the expectation, internal as much as external, that they should be grateful. You chose this. You are living in an exciting place. You have opportunities that others envy. This narrative makes it harder to acknowledge that you are also struggling, and harder still to seek support.

The NHS Every Mind Matters resource on stress describes how chronic, unaddressed stress accumulates in the body and mind, affecting sleep, concentration, physical health, and relationships. The sources of expat stress are often invisible to others: navigating bureaucracy in a second language, managing a sense of professional deskilling, coping with festivals and milestones without the people who matter most, and the persistent low-grade effort of never quite feeling at home.

Over time, these stressors can develop into something more significant: anxiety, depression, or a creeping sense of flatness and disconnection that is hard to name but impossible to ignore. Acknowledging that is not a sign of failure. It is honest.

What the Research Tells Us About Expat Wellbeing

The Expat Insider data consistently shows that the quality of expats’ social lives is the single strongest predictor of their overall happiness abroad, outweighing career satisfaction, financial situation, and even quality of life in their host country. This finding has held year after year across very different national contexts.

The Mental Health Foundation’s research on loneliness reinforces this: chronic loneliness is not simply unpleasant, it has measurable effects on physical health, cognitive function, and mortality risk. Rebuilding a social life in a new country is not a luxury or a nice-to-have: it is one of the most important things an expat can do for their long-term wellbeing.

The research also points clearly to what helps: finding communities built around shared interests or experiences (expat groups, sports, volunteering, creative pursuits); making consistent effort to deepen acquaintances into friendships rather than waiting for closeness to arrive naturally; and, where the emotional load is significant, working with a therapist who understands the specific dynamics of life abroad.

How Therapy Helps People Living Abroad

Therapy for expats is not solely about crisis management. It is often about making sense of a transition that has been more complex than expected, and about working out what you actually want from the life you have built or are building.

In my work with people navigating relocation, the themes that come up most consistently include:

  • Grief for what has been left behind: friends, family, professional identity, a version of home that existed before the move. This grief is real even when the decision to move was the right one.
  • Anxiety about belonging: a persistent sense of being an outsider, of not quite fitting, of not knowing the unspoken rules of a new culture.
  • Relationship strain: the pressure placed on partnerships or marriages when a couple is each other’s primary social world.
  • Identity confusion: uncertainty about who you are when the social context that helped define you no longer exists.
  • Ambivalence about returning: the complicated feeling of not knowing whether to stay or go, or of having been away long enough that “home” no longer feels quite like home either.

These are not small concerns, and they do not resolve themselves simply by waiting long enough. Working through them in therapy provides a space to process what has happened, understand your own responses more clearly, and make more intentional choices about what comes next.

The BACP therapist directory is a useful starting point if you are looking for accredited support. Online therapy, in particular, has made it significantly easier for people living abroad to access a therapist who shares their language and understands their cultural reference points, without being limited by geography.
