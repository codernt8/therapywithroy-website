Understanding Therapy

What Therapy Actually Is (And What It Isn’t)

Roy Lam
MBACP Registered Therapist
6 April 2026 🕒 8 min read

Most people approach therapy with a set of assumptions already in place. Some of those assumptions are useful. A lot of them are not. In my experience, what holds more people back from starting than almost anything else is a fixed idea of what therapy is, what it demands, and what it is supposed to feel like. Very often that idea bears little resemblance to what actually happens.

This post is not a sales pitch for therapy. It is an attempt to give an honest account of what therapy involves, where the most common misconceptions come from, and why some of those myths matter in practical terms. If you have been curious about therapy but have not quite got there yet, I hope this helps you figure out whether it might be right for you, and what to expect if you decide to try it.

Myth One: Therapy Is Only for People in Crisis

This is probably the most persistent and the most unhelpful idea about therapy. It positions therapy as a last resort, something you turn to only when everything else has failed. In reality, therapy is most useful not when you are at your lowest point, but when you have enough capacity and curiosity to actually engage with the process.

Some of the most meaningful work I do with clients is not with people in acute crisis. It is with people who are functioning, holding things together, meeting their responsibilities, and carrying something underneath all of that which they cannot quite name or shift on their own. Persistent low mood, a recurring pattern in relationships, a creeping sense that life is going through the motions rather than actually being lived. These are not dramatic. They are also not nothing.

The American Psychological Association is clear that therapy is appropriate across a wide range of situations, from serious mental health conditions to ordinary life difficulties, career transitions, relationship patterns, and questions about identity and direction. You do not need a diagnosis to benefit from therapy. You need to be willing to show up and think.

Myth Two: The Therapist Will Tell You What to Do

This one comes up a lot. People come to therapy expecting to receive answers, to be told what to decide, to be given a clear prescription for how to fix the thing that is wrong. When that does not happen, it can initially feel frustrating or even like something is being withheld.

Therapy does not work that way, and there are good reasons for that. A therapist who simply told you what to do would be replacing your judgement with theirs, and that is not what good therapy is for. The goal of therapy is to help you understand yourself more clearly, so that the decisions you make, the direction you choose, and the changes you make are genuinely yours.

What I do in a session is not give advice in the conventional sense. I ask questions. I reflect back what I am hearing, including things that might not be fully conscious yet. I notice patterns and name them. I create enough space for you to hear yourself more clearly. The insights that emerge in that process tend to be more durable and more genuinely useful than any advice I could offer, because they come from you.

That said, therapy is not entirely passive either. Depending on the modality, such as CBT, the work may involve specific tools, exercises, or frameworks that you practise between sessions. Mind’s guide to CBT gives a good overview of what that more structured approach involves.

The insights that come from therapy tend to be more durable because they are yours. A therapist who simply handed you the answers would be doing you a disservice.

Myth Three: You Have to Talk About Your Childhood

This idea comes from a particular tradition in therapy, the psychoanalytic one, and it has shaped the popular image of therapy far beyond its actual reach. The image of someone lying on a couch recounting their earliest memories while a silent figure takes notes is not what most therapy looks like in 2026.

Different therapeutic modalities work in different ways. CBT focuses on identifying and shifting unhelpful thought patterns in the present. Emotion-Focused Therapy works with emotional experience, helping clients understand what their feelings are telling them and develop a more flexible relationship with their emotional life. Bowen Family Systems Theory looks at patterns that run through families across generations, not to assign blame but to understand the relational context in which a person developed. These are the three modalities I work with, and none of them require you to spend your sessions excavating your early childhood.

That does not mean your past is irrelevant. Early experiences shape the patterns, beliefs, and ways of relating that we carry into adult life. But the starting point is usually what is happening for you now, what you are finding difficult, what you want to be different, and working backwards from there as and when it is useful.

Myth Four: Therapy Takes Years and Years

This is another legacy of psychoanalytic therapy, where long-term, open-ended work is the norm. For many people and many presenting concerns, this is not what is needed.

The NHS talking therapies overview notes that many evidence-based treatments, including CBT for anxiety and depression, are typically delivered over six to twenty sessions. Some people find a relatively short course of therapy gives them what they need. Others find they want to continue working over a longer period, because the questions they are exploring are deeper or more complex. Neither is wrong.

I tend to work in an open-ended way, meaning we do not set a fixed number of sessions at the outset, but we do keep checking in on how the work is going and whether it is still useful. The pace and duration are shaped by you and your situation, not by a predetermined structure.

What Therapy Actually Involves

At its core, therapy is a structured, confidential relationship in which you have space to explore what is happening for you, with a trained professional whose job is to listen carefully, ask useful questions, and help you understand yourself more fully.

It is not a friendship, though it has warmth in it. It is not a transaction, though it has a professional frame around it. It is not advice, though it offers reflection. The therapeutic relationship itself, the quality of trust, honesty, and genuine attention that develops over time, is often one of the most significant factors in whether therapy is useful. Research consistently shows that the relationship between therapist and client is one of the strongest predictors of good outcomes, independent of the specific modality being used.

Psychology Today’s guide to therapy covers what to look for in a therapist, what a first session typically involves, and what good therapy generally feels like. It is a useful starting point if you are still weighing up the decision.

One thing I would add from my own practice: therapy does not have to feel dramatic to be working. Some of the most significant shifts happen quietly. A reframe that changes how you see a situation. A pattern that becomes visible and, in becoming visible, loses some of its grip. A clearer sense of what you actually want, as distinct from what you have always assumed you should want.

How to Find the Right Therapist

Not every therapist is right for every person. Finding someone you feel comfortable with, whose approach makes sense to you, matters a great deal. The BACP therapist directory is one of the most reliable places to search for accredited therapists in the UK. BACP membership means the therapist is working to a professional code of ethics and has met specific training requirements. The Counselling Directory is another useful resource, with profiles from therapists across a wide range of specialisms.

Most therapists, myself included, offer an initial consultation. This is not a commitment to ongoing work. It is a conversation: a chance to get a sense of the person, ask questions about how they work, and decide whether it feels like the right fit. I offer a free 15 to 30 minute initial consultation for exactly this reason.

I work with clients across the UK and internationally, online via Google Meet. Online therapy has removed one of the most significant practical barriers to starting: the need to travel to a specific location. If you have been putting therapy off partly because of logistics, that barrier may be smaller than you think.
